The family camping checklist that actually works

Packing for a family camping trip is nothing like packing for yourself. This is the gear we'd actually pack for a Midwest state-park weekend with a toddler, sorted by age and by what you truly need first.

A family campsite in the Midwest
The short answerTL;DR

Pack in five layers: a shelter you can stand up in, a warm and level sleep system for every age, a simple camp kitchen, a safety and bug kit built for wooded Midwest parks, and age-specific extras for babies and toddlers. Get those right and the rest is comfort. The single most trip-saving add is a screen canopy for shade, rain, and bug-free meals.

You are not just packing for yourself anymore. You are packing for nap schedules, for the kid who will absolutely fall in the creek, and for a 30-degree swing between a warm Wisconsin afternoon and a cold lakeside morning. This is the list we actually use, grouped so you can go category by category instead of holding a hundred items in your head.

How to think about itPack in layers, by age

The trick that keeps a family pack sane is to build it in two layers. First, a shared base every camper needs, shelter, sleep, kitchen, safety. Then an age layer stacked on top: babies need the most extra gear, toddlers need containment and a lot of spare clothes, school-age kids just need smaller versions of adult gear plus something to do. Pack the base once, then add the age layer per kid, and almost nothing gets forgotten.

Inside each category, sort by priority. Must-haves are the things that make the trip possible or safe. Comfort adds make it pleasant. Fun extras come last, after you have confirmed you own a tent before you pack the marshmallow sticks. If your car is full, cut from the bottom up.

Field note

Start pulling gear out three to four days before you leave, not the night before. A torn tent-pole sleeve or a dead lantern battery is a quick fix at home and a trip-wrecker at the campsite. Lay it out by category, then pack into labeled bins so unpacking (and next year's repack) takes minutes.

The base · Must-havesShelter and sleep

Your shelter and sleep system is the one place not to cut corners with kids, because a cold or wet night ends a trip fast. For the tent, size up: a tent rated for two more people than your group gives you room for gear, a travel crib, and a rainy-morning card game inside. Look for a big rainfly and easy standing height so you are not changing a diaper on your knees.

Shelter & sleep essentials
  • Family tent, rated for 2 more people than your group (a 6-person tent for a family of 4)
  • Tent footprint or ground sheet, keeps the floor dry and protects it from twigs
  • Sleeping bags, temperature-rated for each person, and rated cooler than the forecast for kids
  • Sleeping pads or an air mattress, one per person, off the cold ground
  • Camp pillows (or a stuff sack full of clothes)
  • Extra fleece blankets, the first thing you reach for on a cold Midwest morning
  • Headlamp or lantern plus spare batteries, one light per person

For babies and toddlers, a properly temperature-rated sleep setup matters even more, since little ones cannot regulate their own temperature well. A small travel crib or pack-and-play gives a baby a safe, contained place to sleep, and a fitted crib sheet stretched over an air mattress stops a toddler sliding around all night.

The base · Must-havesThe camp kitchen

Feeding a family outdoors is mostly about keeping food cold, boiling water, and cleaning up without a sink. A good cooler with real ice retention is the workhorse, non-negotiable if you are bringing milk, baby food, or meat for camp dinners. Keep the cooking simple: a two-burner stove or a single burner covers almost every family camp meal, and one-pot dinners save you from washing a pile of pans in cold water.

Camp kitchen essentials
  • Cooler with strong ice retention (plus reusable ice blocks)
  • Camp stove and fuel, with a lighter and backup matches
  • One pot and one pan, a kettle, and a sharp knife
  • Unbreakable plates, bowls, cups and utensils, kid-sized where you can
  • Water jug: plan on a gallon per person per day for drinking, cooking and cleanup
  • Dish tub, biodegradable soap, sponge and quick-dry towel
  • Trash and ziplock bags, plus a sealable bin for food (wildlife-smart at wooded sites)

The base · Must-havesClothing for a Midwest swing

Midwest campgrounds can swing thirty degrees between a hot afternoon and a chilly morning off the water, so layers beat any single warm coat. Pack for one more cold day than the forecast promises, and bring far more kid clothes than feels reasonable, because dirt, water and spilled hot chocolate are guaranteed. Waterproof shoes and a rain layer for every person turn a wet day from a disaster into an adventure.

Clothing per person (kids, multiply)
  • Moisture-wicking base layers and warm mid-layers (fleece or wool), not cotton for warmth
  • Rain jacket for every family member
  • Warm hat and an extra pair of socks for cold nights, even in summer
  • Waterproof or closed-toe shoes plus a second pair that can get wet
  • Three-plus full changes of clothes per kid (they will need them)
  • Swimsuits and a quick-dry towel each for beach and lake days

The base · Must-havesSafety, bugs and first aid

Most Door County and Wisconsin state-park campgrounds are wooded, which means ticks and mosquitoes are part of the deal. A stocked first-aid kit, insect protection, and a tick-removal tool are must-haves, not comfort items, and a nightly tick check on every kid is the routine that keeps the wooded Midwest fun. Print an emergency card with the campground office and nearest hospital, since cell signal at a state park is never guaranteed.

Safety & first-aid kit
  • First-aid kit: bandages, antiseptic, tweezers, pain relievers (adult and children's)
  • Tick-removal tool and after-bite or sting relief
  • Insect repellent (a version formulated for babies for the little ones) and sunscreen
  • Any prescription meds in a labeled waterproof bag
  • Printed emergency contacts: campground office, nearest hospital, family numbers
  • Whistle and a small light for each kid, in case they wander

The age layerBabies and toddlers, the gear most lists forget

This is the category that catches new camping parents out, because a baby or toddler roughly doubles your gear. The two problems to solve are sleep (a safe, warm, contained place) and containment (a way to keep a mobile toddler out of the fire and the road while you cook). A pack-and-play solves the first; a fold-up travel high chair or a simple playpen solves the second and buys you both hands at dinner.

From our family

Camping with a one-year-old, the thing that saved us was a screen shelter over the picnic table: shade for naps, a bug-free zone for meals, and somewhere dry to gather when it drizzled. We also brought a small foldable potty for the toddler, because a pit toilet in the dark is a hard sell at age three.

Baby & toddler add-on
  • Travel crib or pack-and-play and a warm, rated baby sleeping bag or sack
  • Fold-up travel high chair or a small playpen for containment at camp
  • Portable potty or a toilet-seat insert, plus far more diapers and wipes than you think
  • Sun hat, baby-safe sunscreen and infant insect repellent
  • Baby carrier for trailside walks a stroller cannot handle
  • Familiar comfort item, the blanket or toy that means sleep at home

The age layerOlder kids

Once kids are out of the toddler stage they mostly need smaller versions of your gear plus a job and something to do. Giving each kid their own headlamp and small daypack turns them into campers instead of passengers, and low-tech entertainment beats screens every time: a deck of cards, a ball, glow sticks after dark, and a simple nature scavenger hunt will hold attention for surprisingly long stretches. Save the elaborate stuff for a rainy-day backup.

The 90 percent fixThe little things that save the trip

After the big gear, a handful of tiny, cheap items quietly solve most of the problems that come up at a campsite. Pack these regardless of your kids' ages, they weigh almost nothing and you will use every one.

The trip-savers
  • A screen canopy or shade tent, the single best add for shade, rain and bug-free meals
  • Duct tape and paracord, for the tent, the tarp, and everything you did not plan for
  • Extra ziplock bags and a roll of paper towel
  • Baby wipes, useful at every age for hands, faces, gear and spills
  • A doormat for the tent door, the simplest way to keep sand and mud out of the sleep space
  • A power bank, since state-park sites rarely have outlets

Where it comes togetherCamping in the Midwest with this list

This checklist is built for the kind of trip we take most: a weekend at a Wisconsin or Midwest state park, tent up, toddler in tow. Those sites are gloriously simple, which is exactly why the list matters, there is no camp store to save you, so what you bring is what you have. If Door County is on your list, our Door County family camping guide covers which parks and campgrounds suit families, and our full Door County guide plans the rest of the trip around it.

This is a general packing framework, not gear-specific advice for a particular child or medical need. Check the temperature rating on sleep gear against your actual forecast, confirm campground rules (fires, food storage, quiet hours) on the park's official site, and pack for one degree colder and one day longer than you expect.

Before you go

What do you actually need for family tent camping with kids?

At a minimum: a family tent sized up by two people, a temperature-rated sleeping bag and pad for each person, a cooler and a camp stove, weather layers with a rain jacket for everyone, and a first-aid and bug kit. With a baby or toddler, add a travel crib, a containment option like a playpen or travel high chair, and far more diapers, wipes and spare clothes than you would guess.

What camping gear do babies and toddlers need that adults don't?

The big extras are a safe contained sleep setup (a travel crib or pack-and-play plus a rated baby sleeping bag), a way to contain a mobile toddler at camp (a playpen or fold-up high chair), a portable potty, and a lot more diapers, wipes and clothing changes. A baby carrier, sun hat, baby-safe sunscreen and infant insect repellent round it out.

How do you keep kids warm camping on a cold Midwest night?

Layer their sleep system: a sleeping pad or air mattress to get them off the cold ground, a bag rated cooler than the forecast, warm base layers, a hat and dry socks for sleeping, and a fleece blanket on top. Kids lose heat faster than adults, so plan for one degree colder than the forecast, especially near Lake Michigan where mornings run cold even in summer.

What is the one piece of camping gear families forget most?

A screen canopy or shade tent. It is the item experienced camping families swear by, because it gives you shade for naps, a dry place to gather when it rains, and a bug-free zone for meals, all of which matter far more with kids than with adults. After that, the most-forgotten small items are a tick-removal tool, extra socks, and enough spare kid clothes.

How far ahead should I start packing for a family camping trip?

Three to four days ahead, not the night before. Pulling gear out early gives you time to spot a torn tent-pole sleeve, a dead lantern battery, or an outgrown rain jacket while you can still fix it at home. Lay everything out by category, then pack into labeled bins so setup and next year's repack are quick.
